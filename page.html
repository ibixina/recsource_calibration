The short answer

The layout can be recovered reliably with enough devices and slow enough motion. Absolute position can't be, unless something stays put. With 6 receivers, 6 sources and timing noise of 1% of the arena, the layout was correct for 100% of readings when every device moved up to 3% of the arena between readings, and 95% at 10%.

  • How many you need. On a surface, at least 3 receivers and 3 sources. 3 × 3 is ambiguous, with up to 4 exact fits found. Every count tested from 3 × 4 up had one. In 3D, 4 × 6 or 5 × 5 fixes the layout locally, and one exact fit starts at 4 × 7 or 5 × 6.
  • One snapshot. At 1% timing noise on random layouts: 3 × 4 solved 65%, 4 × 4 82%, 5 × 5 97%, 6 × 6 100%.
  • Speed is the main limit. At 4 × 4 with everything moving, correct readings fall from 97% at 1% of the arena per reading to 90% at 3% and 53% at 10%. Moving one device at a time holds up far better: 100%, 97% and 95%.
  • The typical failure is a mirror flip. A mirror image gives identical readings, so once the layout flips, the readings can't correct it. At 4 × 4 and 10% per reading, 32% of readings were mirrored. Three pinned sources took the mirrored share from 31% to 5%, and correct readings from 55% to 87%, in a separate comparison.
  • Absolute position drifts. The group's net motion leaves no trace in the readings. At 6 × 6 with everything moving, median position error reached 42% of the arena after 1,000 readings. One pinned source gave 23%, and two gave 1%. At 4 × 4 two pins can still leave a mirror image across the line between them, so three is the safe number: 90th-percentile error of 50% with two, 1.8% with three.
  • The system mostly can't tell when it's wrong. False alarms stay rare, under 2% for 4 × 4 and larger at speeds up to 3%, but most wrong readings also pass the fit check. At 4 × 4 and 3% per reading, 4 of the 5 points of wrong readings went unnoticed, and 3 × 3 has no spare readings to check with at all.
  • Signal strength is much harder than timing. A single snapshot almost never works at 20% distance noise (7% solved at best). Tracking at 6 × 6 can still work by averaging, if motion is slow: at 30% noise it was correct 99% of the time at 0.1% per reading, 52% at 0.3%, and 5% at 1%.
  • Being told the starting layout matters little from 4 × 4 up. Correct readings were 96% told versus 97% worked out, at 1% per reading. It matters more at 3 × 4: 85% versus 77%. A system that works it out still has no absolute position or handedness of its own. These tests line it up with reality once at the start.

Before noise matters, there may be too few readings. These counts use perfect readings on random layouts: each cell gives the most distinct layouts found that reproduce every reading exactly. Rotated, shifted and mirrored copies count as the same layout.

High correct rates still hide brief losses. At 4 × 4 and 1% per reading, 97% of readings were correct, yet only 45% of the 300-reading runs stayed under 5% the whole way. Recovery matters. When readings stop fitting, the system re-solves from scratch. Turning that off dropped 4 × 4 at 3% per reading from 86% to 62%, and 6 × 6 at 10% from 98% to 63%. Where you place things matters

Single-snapshot results at 1% timing noise. Solved means layout error under 5%. The bound is the median error the geometry allows locally.

Random placement is already good, and surrounding the receivers with sources did no better: at 4 × 4 it solved 67% against 87% for random. Two placements fail outright. With sources in a line, each receiver can flip across that line without changing a reading, so the local bound looks fine while the solver is often wrong. Two distant clusters barely constrain how the groups are arranged. A bound of "none" means the exact geometry has a first-order blind spot. Parallel lines fail because of it, but devices exactly on one circle still solve well from 4 × 4 up.

What these numbers assume

Clean signal model

Readings depend only on distance, and the system knows that law and the noise level exactly. Multipath, blocked paths, outliers and uneven device gains aren't modeled, so real hardware will do worse than these numbers.

A full table every time

Every receiver hears every source, tells the sources apart, and reads them all at the same moment. Timing assumes synchronized clocks. Unsynchronized clocks would add unknowns.

Scale

Error is RMS per device as a share of the arena side, so 5% is 50 cm in a 10 m room. "Correct" means under 5%, so a wrong reading can be just over that line. The strength dB figures assume a path-loss exponent of 2.

Real-world orientation

As rules of thumb not tested here, ultra-wideband ranging is often quoted near 10 cm, about 1% of a 10 m room, and indoor Bluetooth or Wi-Fi strength often varies by several dB, which is past the 30% column.

Sample sizes

Snapshot cells use 200 random layouts, or 100 in 3D. Tracking cells use 60 runs of 300 readings, or 30 runs at 8 × 8. A flip lasts for the rest of a run, so treat tracking percentages as good to about 10 points.

Check it yourself

The solver's error matches the Cramér-Rao bound within 7% at low noise, in the test suite. Run node --test engine.test.js for tests, node sweep.js for this data (about 20 minutes), and node build.js to rebuild the page.
